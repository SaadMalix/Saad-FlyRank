Abstract

This project asks which content pages, using only what is known on the 15th of a month, are most likely to lose even more search impressions over the rest of that month, so an editor can review them first. I built one row per page from daily Search Console data in the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse (Feb, Mar and Jun 2026), with seven first-half-of-month signals and a label that only counts further decline from each page's latest week. A Random Forest was compared against a transparent hand-written rule and a one-feature momentum ranking, using client-grouped and time-aware splits, and finally scored once on a sealed month (June 2026) of clients it had never seen. On that sealed test, the model's top 50 pages declined further 74% of the time against a 49% base rate, but by the top 500 it only tied the rule and its whole-list ROC AUC was near chance (0.52). The result is a small, directional edge at the very top of the list, useful as decision support for a short weekly review queue and not as a site-wide score.

1. The problem: a short review list, a very long site

Picture an SEO editor on the 15th of the month with time to review about 50 pages, out of tens of thousands that are live. Some pages are about to lose visibility. Most are just wobbling. The editor needs a ranked list that puts the pages likely to keep falling at the top, with a plain reason for each one.

The obvious approach is a hand-written rule, such as "page one, below-average CTR, a few months old". The interesting tension, and most of this paper, is about how easy it is to fool yourself when measuring whether a model beats that rule. My first version reported a model that looked 93% precise. After an audit, the honest number turned out to be much smaller. This page reports the honest one, and explains where the big one came from.

2. Data

  • Source: the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (pseudonymized, hosted on Hugging Face), queried in place with DuckDB.
  • Tables: fact_content_daily_performance (one row = one page on one day, Search Console + Analytics) and dim_content (used only for each page's creation date).
  • Windows: February and March 2026 for development. June 2026, the final month of the panel, was kept sealed and scored once at the end.
  • Unit: one page per month. Signals come from days 1–15 and the outcome from day 16 to month end.
  • Kept: rows where gsc_data_available is true. Pages need at least 50 impressions in days 1–15 and 25 in days 8–15.
  • Excluded: GA4/sessions/AI-referral columns (only filled for some clients and days; zero-filled rows read as "no traffic"), content-update and optimization dates (mostly dated after the decision day, i.e. future information), and all IDs and hashes as features.

3. Methodology

Features (days 1–15 only)

FeatureMeaning
log_imp_h1log of impressions
ctr_h1clicks ÷ impressions

active_days_h1days with at least one impression
momentum_h1daily impressions in days 8–15 ÷ days 1–7

ctr_gapCTR minus the median CTR of pages at a similar position

Label: only count further decline

A page is labelled 1 if its average daily impressions from day 16 to month end fall below 80% of its days 8–15 average.

An earlier version compared the second half with the whole first half. That quietly counted a drop that had already happened in week 2 as a "future" decline. So "impressions fell in week 2" (momentum) looked like a superb predictor, even though it was mostly re-reading the past. The chart shows the difference.

Baselines

  • Hand-written rule (frozen before modeling): 2 points for a below-median CTR on page one, 1 point for being 31–180 days old, weighted by log impressions. Both signals were checked with bucket tables first: CTR-vs-position was confirmed on page one, and age was mixed (risk peaks at 3–6 months).
  • Momentum only: rank by the lowest week-2/week-1 ratio. No model.
  • Random picking: the base rate.

Model and validation

A Random Forest (300 trees, minimum 20 pages per leaf, seed 42), chosen because it handles non-linear signals without tuning. It was checked three ways, from least to most strict:

  1. Client-grouped 5-fold on March: every test client is unseen in training. A random row split was tried and dropped: it inflated ROC AUC from 0.69 to 0.76 by letting the model memorize each client.
  2. Time-aware: train on February, test on March.
  3. Sealed final test: train on February + March, test on June pages from 12 held-out clients (32,805 pages, zero client overlap). Run once.

Leakage checks

  • Features use days 1–15 only, and only the label uses day 16 onward (asserted in code).
  • Deliberate-leak test: adding the label-window impressions as a feature pushes ROC AUC from 0.61 to 0.96. The test harness catches leaks, and that column is never a feature.
  • No product flags or post-decision dates are used. No single feature scores suspiciously well.

During development (March, grouped by client) the model looked better: P@50 0.60 vs a 0.30 base rate and AUC 0.61. Trained on February and tested on March, most of that edge disappeared (P@50 0.46, AUC 0.56, no better than the rule). The sealed June test brings back a top-of-list edge but no whole-list skill. The steady pattern across all three is a modest edge in the first few dozen pages, and nothing reliable below that.

What the model leans on (permutation importance, sealed test): average position and page age first, then active days and the CTR gap. Momentum, which looked like the star under the flawed label, barely matters here.

5. Limitations & honest framing

These are observed, directional results for decision support. They are not a forecast and not a causal finding.
  • No causal claims. Nothing here shows why pages decline, or that editing a page prevents decline. Recommendations are prompts for human review.
  • Nothing about Google's ranking algorithm. The inputs are observed Search Console aggregates, not ranking factors.
  • The edge is narrow. Only the top ~50–100 pages beat the base rate on the sealed test. The whole-list score is near chance and shouldn't be used to sort a whole site.
  • It doesn't transfer well between months. February → March lost most of the development edge, and the base rate ranged from 22% to 45% across months.
  • Short, noisy windows. Two-week comparisons react to holidays, one-off spikes and portfolio-wide swings.
  • Coverage. Only Search Console–tracked pages with enough impressions. New, tiny or untracked pages are out of scope.
  • One label definition, one seed, one held-out client set. With 12 test clients, a few large clients weigh heavily. The top of the June queue came from 8 clients, one of them holding 19 of the 50.
  • Anonymized data. Pseudonymized clients and pages. No client can be identified, and none should be inferred.

6. Ranked recommendations (the action playbook)

For a live cycle, the model is trained on all February + March pages and scores every page on the 15th. Each page gets a tier, one reason code and one action. The June 2026 cycle is shown below with outcomes known after the fact.

TierPagesObserved further decline
  1. Review only Tier A (top 50) each cycle, and cap each client at about 10 pages. This is where the measured edge is (70% vs 45% in June). The cap stops one publishing batch from filling the whole list.
  2. young_page_settling (31–180 days, deep positions): check content completeness against the pages outranking it. This was the most common Tier A reason in June.
  3. ctr_below_position (page one, CTR below its position's median): review the title and snippet against the query intent before touching the body.
  4. already_sliding (week-2 impressions already falling): review for staleness: facts, dates, missing subtopics.
  5. rising_at_deep_position: monitor, don't edit. A gain deep in the results often fades on its own, and editing it now wastes effort.
  6. model_risk_only: manual review. The model sees risk that no simple check explains, so a person judges it.
  7. Never automate edits from this score, and re-check it monthly. If the Tier A hit rate falls to the base rate for two cycles in a row, retrain or retire it.

7. Reproducibility

  • Repository: github.com/SaadMalix/Saad-FlyRank
  • Capstone notebook (produces every number and chart on this page): work/notebooks/capstone.ipynb
  • Shared data code (frame, label, rule, splits): work/scripts/lane_data.py
  • The path that led here: framing · data contract · baseline · first model · validation audit (where the label artifact was found)
  • To rerun: request access to the warehouse release on Hugging Face, log in with a read token (hf auth login), install requirements.txt, then run the capstone notebook top to bottom. Seed 42, rows sorted before splitting; scikit-learn 1.9.0. No data files are committed; the ranked queue is regenerated locally.
